How the machine works

In the early 1980s Peter Struycken was asked for a portrait of Queen Beatrix for the everyday stamp. He wanted the head of state built from the least meaningful element he knew — the distance between points — and asked the pattern recognition group of TU Delft (F.C.A. Groen and P.W. Verbeek) for help. Their program works in two phases.

What Trix adds

Other walks. The meander is one of many space-filling walks. A Hilbert curve keeps the carried error close in both directions; a spiral leaves fingerprint whorls; a random order shows why the walk matters at all.

Territories that follow the tone. Instead of one fixed territory, every dot claims a share of the local spacing (1/√density). Shadows stay dense, highlights get generous room, and every tone becomes even blue noise. Moves that only reduce an overlap are allowed, so dots packed too tightly by phase 1 can drift apart.

Multi-scale steps and annealing. Step lengths are drawn as max·u², so marbles in crowded shadows still find the little room next to them, and steps can shrink pass by pass like a cooling metal.

Seeds. Every random choice comes from a seed you can name. The same seed gives the same portrait, dot for dot, a little like a printing plate number. It is printed in the microtext of the stamp.
